Australia · Heatwave patterns

Australian heatwaves

How Australia’s heatwaves are changing in frequency, duration and intensity.

Heatwaves have more than one measure. Start with how many days Australia spends in heatwave conditions, then explore where they occur, how long they last and how intense they can be.

The national charts cover 1980–2025, with 2025 available to 29 August. The maps provide a closer look at 2020–2025.

07 · The intensity distribution

Are intense heatwave days becoming more common?

These ridges compare the mix of intensities among heatwave days across Australian land grid cells. Each period is normalised to the same total density, so ridge height shows relative concentration, not the number of heatwave days. EHF describes heat relative to local conditions and is expressed in °C², not air temperature.

What to notice: The pattern is not a steady rise. The share of positive EHF observations at or above 12 °C² increases from 9.4% in the 1980s to 15.5% in the 2010s, then falls to 8.0% in 2020–2025. This is a change in the intensity mix among heatwave days; these normalised curves alone cannot establish whether intense days occur more often across all days.

Read from the 1980s at the top to 2020–2025 at the bottom. The symlog axis expands lower EHF values while retaining the full observed tail; equal horizontal distances do not represent equal intensity changes. Heights share a common density scale in original EHF units; areas on this nonlinear display are not probabilities. Hover for period, intensity and density.

All available positive grid-cell-day observations are pooled within each period, using the existing Australian land mask. Common 0.5 °C² bins and a shared 1 °C² smoothing bandwidth approximate the distribution shape. Years with more heatwave observations contribute more to the pooled curve. The 12 °C² comparison benchmark is the upper edge of the bin containing the 1980s 90th percentile, not an official severe or extreme threshold.

Incomplete 1979 is excluded. The last period covers six years, 2020–2025; 2025 ends on 29 August. Available dates are used without filling gaps or adjusting to a full year.

Source: CSIRO daily EHF, processed offline using the existing Natural Earth Australian land mask. Processed densities · Processing, coverage and distribution summaries.

08 · City heatwave profiles

Different cities experience different kinds of heatwaves

Follow each city’s line across four measures to see its heatwave profile. Every vertical axis is independently normalised from the lowest to the highest value among these eight locations. Positions compare cities within a measure; they do not imply that days and EHF have the same units. Axis ticks and tooltips show the original values.

What to notice: Darwin has the most average annual heatwave days (23.7) and the longest average annual runs (10.8 days), but the lowest mean and maximum EHF among these locations. Adelaide has the highest mean positive EHF (15.1 °C²), while Melbourne has the highest maximum EHF (70.7 °C²). Longer or more frequent heatwaves do not necessarily have higher EHF.

The period includes partial 2025 data to 29 August without adjustment to a full year. Runs may be split at annual file boundaries. These eight selected locations are not a national ranking.

09 · BOM long-term temperature context

How has Adelaide’s temperature distribution shifted?

BOM ACORN-SAT provides independent, long-term temperature context for the CSIRO heatwave analysis. These violins compare Adelaide’s daily maximum temperatures across five historical periods. Wider parts show temperatures that were more common within that period; the dark mark shows the median, with half of recorded days above and half below it.

What to notice: The median is 20.7 °C in 1910–1929, compared with 21.3 °C in 2000–2019 and 21.4 °C in 2020–2024. The upper tail does not rise steadily: 5.8% of recorded days reach at least 35 °C in 2000–2019, compared with 3.9% in 1910–1929 and 4.2% in 2020–2024. The final period covers only five years.

All violins share the same temperature scale and smoothing. Width represents relative density, so a wider violin does not mean more observations. Compare the upper tips to explore the hotter tail. 10 · Understanding the data

Methodology and limitations

Reading the measures. Heatwave days count days with a positive Excess Heat Factor (EHF); duration counts consecutive positive-EHF days. EHF is an intensity index expressed in °C², not air temperature. The intensity map shows the maximum EHF and the mean across positive-EHF days during 2020–2025.

Spatial averages. The national line shows average annual heatwave days per grid cell, not a total across the country. For state duration, each grid cell’s annual longest run is averaged within its state, then across years. Cells and years receive equal weight. State averages hide local differences; grey areas have no represented grid cells.

Duration distribution. The boxplot uses the same equal-cell annual state means as the duration map, but keeps each year separate for 1980–2025. Each state contributes 46 annual values, with equal weight per year. Boxes show the middle 50% of those values, not variation between heatwave events. Unmatched coastal cells are excluded using the same full ABS boundary join; the ACT is represented by 17 grid centres.

Frequency comparison. Annual counts of days with positive EHF are averaged equally across grid cells in each state, then equally across the ten years in each comparison period. The full ABS boundary join excludes unmatched coastal cells; this is not area- or population-weighted. Change is the 2016–2025 average minus the 1980–1989 average, expressed in days. Sorting uses its absolute magnitude. Partial 2025 counts are included without annualisation.

Intensity ridges. Finite positive daily EHF values are pooled across land grid cells and available dates within each period, with equal weight per grid-cell-day and no area or population weighting. Shared 0.5 °C² histogram bins are smoothed with a Gaussian bandwidth of 1 °C², reflected at zero. Each curve is normalised to unit area in original EHF units. This compares conditional intensity distributions, not the incidence of intense days across all days. Counts reconcile with the existing annual national summaries.

City profiles. Each metric is independently min–max normalised across the eight selected locations: (value − minimum) / (maximum − minimum). A position of zero indicates the lowest observed city value, not zero days or EHF. If all cities share a value, the metric is placed at the midpoint. Original values and aggregation are retained from the existing city intensity dataset.

BOM temperature distributions. Every valid daily maximum temperature in the five selected periods is retained, including repeated values. No sampling, binning or seasonal adjustment is applied. Vega-Lite estimates each probability density using a shared 1 °C Gaussian bandwidth on a 0–55 °C range, then mirrors it into a violin. Medians and hot-day shares use original observations, not smoothed curves. Periods have different lengths and some missing dates; the five-year 2020–2024 sample should be interpreted with that shorter coverage in mind.

Monthly activity. Daily CSIRO EHF is processed offline using the existing Australian land mask. For each month, positive-EHF days are counted per cell, then averaged equally across cells with finite EHF on every available date. This is not an area- or population-weighted measure. Monthly counts use available dates without filling gaps or adjusting to a full month. 1980–2024 have complete daily coverage; 2025 ends on 29 August. The seasonal takeaway uses complete years, and the annual average rises from 12.3 days per cell in the 1980s to 17.3 in the 2010s. Monthly coverage and aggregation details.

Coverage and boundaries. The maps cover 2020–2025, not the full 2020s. Results use available dates without annualisation (adjustment to a full year); 2025 may be incomplete. Heatwaves crossing yearly file boundaries may be split, shortening the recorded duration. Unmatched coastal cells are excluded from state summaries.

Selected locations. Each intensity location uses the nearest valid CSIRO land-grid centre, 2.3–6.9 km from the city centre. These locations do not identify the most intense grid cell in Australia. The maps compare places in a recent period; they do not show changes in duration or intensity over time.

Further detail: state aggregation metadata. Prepared for FIT3179 Data Visualisation 2.
